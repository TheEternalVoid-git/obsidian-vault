# 05 Pure Mathematics 2: Differentiation and Integration

## 1. Principles of Differentiation

Differentiation measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function. For a function $y = f(x)$, the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or $f'(x)$ represents the gradient function of the curve.

### The Fundamental Rule
For any real constant $n$:
$$\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$$

### Linearity of Differentiation
When differentiating a polynomial term by term, constants are preserved and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives:
$$ \frac{d}{dx}[af(x) + bg(x)] = af'(x) + bg'(x) $$


### Algebraic Preprocessing Required for IAL Exams
Functions must often be manipulated into an index form $ax^n$ before differentiation can occur.
* **Fractional Terms:** Convert variables in denominators using negative indices:
  $$\frac{a}{x^n} = ax^{-n}$$
* **Radical Terms:** Convert roots into fractional exponents:
  $$\sqrt[m]{x^n} = x^{\frac{n}{m}}$$
* **Expanded Polynomial Quotients:** Separate rational expressions term-by-term:
  $$\frac{Ax^3 + Bx^2 + C}{x} = Ax^2 + Bx + Cx^{-1}$$

## 2. Applications of Differentiation

### Tangents and Normals
The derivative provides the gradient of the tangent to the curve at a specific point $P(x_1, y_1)$.
* **Gradient of the Tangent ($m_t$):** 
  $$m_t = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right\vert_{x = x_1}$$
* **Equation of the Tangent:**
  $$y - y_1 = m_t(x - x_1)$$
* **Gradient of the Normal ($m_n$):** The normal is perpendicular to the tangent line.
  $$m_n = -\frac{1}{m_t} = -\frac{1}{\left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right\vert_{x = x_1}}$$
* **Equation of the Normal:**
  $$y - y_1 = m_n(x - x_1)$$

### Increasing and Decreasing Functions
* A function is strictly **increasing** on an interval if its gradient is non-negative everywhere in that interval:
  $$\frac{dy}{dx} \ge 0$$
* A function is strictly **decreasing** on an interval if its gradient is non-positive everywhere in that interval:
  $$\frac{dy}{dx} \le 0$$

### Stationary Points and Local Extrema
Stationary points occur where the gradient of the function is flat:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$

To determine the nature of a stationary point at $x = c$, compute the second derivative $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$:

| Value of Second Derivative | Nature of Stationary Point | Graphic Behavior                                                                                       |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} < 0$    | Local Maximum              | Curve is convex (down $\cap$)                                                                          |
| $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} > 0$    | Local Minimum              | Curve is concave (up $\cup$)                                                                           |
| $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 0$    | Inconclusive               | Must test the gradient $\frac{dy}{dx}$ immediately to the left ($c-\epsilon$) and right ($c+\epsilon$) |

If $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 0$ and the gradient does not change sign across the point, it is either a point of inflection or a standard local extremum (min or max).

## 3. Principles of Integration

Integration is the fundamental inverse operation of differentiation. 

### The Fundamental Indefinite Integral Rule
For any real constant $n \neq -1$:
$$\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + c$$
Where $c$ represents the arbitrary constant of integration. This constant must always be written for indefinite integrals.

### Determining the Constant of Integration
To calculate a unique value for $c$, substitute a known coordinate point $(x_0, y_0)$ that lies on the integrated curve into the resolved equation $y = F(x) + c$.

## 4. Definite Integration and Area Under Curves

### Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
A definite integral evaluates the net area bounded by the curve, the x-axis, and the vertical boundary lines $x = a$ and $x = b$:
$$\int_{a}^{b} f'(x) \, dx = [f(x)]_{a}^{b} = f(b) - f(a)$$

### Area Calculations
The exact area $A$ bounded by the curve $y = f(x)$, the x-axis, and lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ is given by:
$$A = \int_{a}^{b} y \, dx$$

#### Areas Below the X-axis
If a curve dips below the x-axis within the interval $[a, b]$, the evaluation of $\int_{a}^{b} y \, dx$ outputs a negative numerical result. 
* **Handling Split Regions:** When calculating the total physical area bounded by a curve that crosses the x-axis at $x = c$ (where $a < c < b$), integrate each region independently:
  $$\text{Total Area} = \left\vert \int_{a}^{c} y \, dx \right\vert + \left\vert \int_{c}^{b} y \, dx \right\vert$$

#### Area Bounded Between a Curve and a Straight Line
To calculate the area enclosed between a curve $y_1 = f(x)$ and a straight line $y_2 = g(x)$ intersecting at boundaries $x = a$ and $x = b$:
$$A = \int_{a}^{b} (y_{\text{upper}} - y_{\text{lower}}) \, dx$$

## 5. Numerical Integration: The Trapezium Rule

When a function cannot be integrated analytically using P2 techniques, the area under the curve is approximated by splitting the region into $n$ vertical strips of equal width $h$.

### Formula
$$\int_{a}^{b} y \, dx \approx \frac{1}{2}h \left[ y_0 + y_n + 2(y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_{n-1}) \right]$$

### Parameter Definitions
* **Number of Coordinates/Ordinates:** Let $N$ be the number of vertical $y$-lines. $N = n + 1$.
* **Strip Width ($h$):** 
  $$h = \frac{b - a}{n}$$

### Under-estimates vs Over-estimates
The mathematical nature of the approximation depends entirely on the curvature (convexity) of the function:
* **Over-estimate:** Occurs when the curve is convex down / concave up ($\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} > 0$). The top chords of the physical trapezia pass above the real curve line.
* **Under-estimate:** Occurs when the curve is convex up / concave down ($\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} < 0$). The top chords of the physical trapezia cut underneath the real curve line.
