# Mechanics

## 1. Core Scalar and Vector Quantities

Physical quantities are classified based on their directional dependence.
* **Scalar Quantities:** Possess magnitude only. Examples include distance, speed, time, mass, energy, and work done.
* **Vector Quantities:** Possess both magnitude and a defined direction. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, and momentum.

### Vector Resolution
Any coplanar vector $V$ acting at an angle $	heta$ relative to the horizontal plane can be resolved into two mutually perpendicular, independent components:
* **Horizontal Component:** 
  $$V_x = V \cos(\theta)$$
* **Vertical Component:** 
  $$V_y = V \sin(\theta)$$

### Vector Addition and Resultants
When multiple forces or velocities act simultaneously on a single point, their combined effect is represented by a single resultant vector.
* **Perpendicular Vectors:** The magnitude of the resultant vector $R$ formed by two orthogonal vectors $A$ and $B$ is calculated using Pythagoras' theorem:
  $$R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$$
* **Directional Orientation:** The angle $\phi$ relative to vector $A$ is given by:
  $$\phi = tan^{-1}\left(\frac{B}{A}
\right)$$

## 2. Kinematics of Linear Motion

Linear kinematics models the movement of an object along a straight line under uniform constant acceleration.

### Core Kinematic Variables
* **Displacement ($s$):** The straight-line distance from a fixed reference point in a specified direction.
* **Initial Velocity ($u$):** The instantaneous velocity at time $t = 0$.
* **Final Velocity ($v$):** The instantaneous velocity at time $t$.
* **Acceleration ($a$):** The uniform rate of change of velocity.
* **Time ($t$):** The duration of the observed motion.

### Derivation of the Equations of Motion (SUVAT)
The five foundational kinematic equations apply strictly under conditions where acceleration $a$ remains perfectly uniform.

#### Equation 1: From the Definition of Acceleration
By definition, acceleration is the rate of change of velocity:
$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$
Rearranging directly yields:
$$v = u + at$$

#### Equation 2: From Average Velocity
Displacement is the product of the average velocity and the total elapsed time:
$$s = \left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)t$$

#### Equation 3: Eliminating Final Velocity ($v$)
Substitute Equation 1 ($v = u + at$) into Equation 2:
$$s = \left(\frac{u + (u + at)}{2}\right)t$$
$$s = \left(\frac{2u + at}{2}
\right)t$$
$$s = ut + rac{1}{2}at^2$$

#### Equation 4: Eliminating Initial Velocity ($u$)
Alternatively, substituting $u = v - at$ into Equation 2 yields:
$$s = vt -\frac{1}{2}at^2$$

#### Equation 5: Eliminating Time ($t$)
Rearrange Equation 1 to isolate time:
$$t = \frac{v - u}{a}$$
Substitute this expression for $t$ into Equation 2:
$$s = \left(\frac{v + u}{2}
\right)\left(\frac{v - u}{a}
\right)$$
$$s = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2a}$$
Rearranging to isolate $v^2$ yields:
$$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$

### Graphical Analysis of Motion

#### Displacement-Time ($s-t$) Graphs
* The gradient at any given point represents the instantaneous velocity:
  $$\text{Gradient} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = v$$
* A straight diagonal line indicates a uniform constant velocity.
* A curved line represents accelerating or decelerating motion.

## 3. Projectile Motion

Projectile motion involves the two-dimensional flight of an unpowered object through a uniform gravitational field. Air resistance is assumed to be negligible for all core IAL modeling calculations. 

Horizontal and vertical components of motion are entirely independent and must be analyzed separately.

---

### Horizontal Projectile Motion
This case models an object launched horizontally from an initial vertical height $h$ with an initial velocity   $u$.

![[screenshot-2026-10-07_11-57-39_54273.png]]

>[!warning] Note!
>Impact Velocity $v_{\text {final}} \neq 0$ ; It is the velocity  of the object  at the instant it hits the ground, not the velocity immediately after hitting the ground


#### Governing Equations & Quantitative Resolution
* **Horizontal Dynamics:** Acceleration is zero ($a_x = 0$). Horizontal velocity stays constant throughout the entire flight path. The horizontal displacement (range, $x$) is given by:
  $$v_x = u$$
  $$s = ut$$
* **Vertical Dynamics:** Initial vertical velocity is zero ($u_y = 0$). The object accelerates uniformly downward under gravity ($a_y = -g = -9.81	ext{ ms}^{-2}$). Taking downward as positive for a cliff calculation simplifies the expressions to:
  $$v_y = gt$$
  $$h = \frac{gt^2}{2}$$

#### Protocol to Find Core Flight Values
1. **Time of Flight ($t$):** Calculate directly from the initial vertical height, independent of the launch velocity:
   $$t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$$
2. **Horizontal Range ($s$):** Substitute the calculated flight time into the horizontal displacement equation:
   $$s = u 	\times \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$$
3. **Impact Velocity ($v_{\text{final}}$):** Calculate the final vertical velocity component at impact ($v_y = gt$). Combine it with the unchanging horizontal component using Pythagoras' theorem to find the true velocity magnitude:
   $$v_{\text{final}} = \sqrt{u^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{u^2 + (gt)^2}$$
4. **Impact Angle ($\theta_{\text{final}}$):** Calculate the landing vector orientation relative to the horizontal plane:
   $$\theta_{\text{final}} = 	tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|v_y|}{u}
\right)$$

### Vertical Projectile Motion (Linear Projection)
This case models an object projected vertically upward from a ground line ($\theta = 90^\circ$). Motion is strictly constrained to a single dimensional axis.

![[screenshot-2026-10-07_12-04-17_57743.png]]

#### Governing Equations & Quantitative Resolution
* **Horizontal Dynamics:** No components exist ($v_x = 0$, $x = 0$).
* **Vertical Dynamics:** Initial velocity is non-zero ($u_y = u$). The object experiences constant deceleration by gravity on its ascent ($a_y = -g = -9.81\text{ ms}^{-2}$):
  $$v_y = u - gt$$
  $$s = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$
  $$v_y^2 = u^2 - 2gs$$

#### Protocol to Find Core Flight Values
1. **Time to Peak ($t_{\text{peak}}$):** Set final vertical velocity at the absolute apex to zero ($v_y = 0$):
   $$0 = u - gt_{\text{peak}} \implies t_{\text{peak}} = \frac{u}{g}$$
2. **Maximum Height ($y_{\text{max}}$):** Calculate using the time-independent equation where final velocity equals zero:
   $$0 = u^2 - 2gy_{\text{max}} \implies y_{\text{max}} = \frac{u^2}{2g}$$
3. **Total Time of Flight ($t_{\text{total}}$):** For a projectile returning to its exact launch height, the trajectory is completely symmetrical. The total time of flight is twice the time taken to reach the peak:
   $$t_{\text{total}} = 2t_{\text{peak}} =\frac{2u}{g}$$

### Classification 3: Angular Projectile Motion (Oblique Projection)
This case models an object launched from ground level with an initial velocity $u$ at an arbitrary angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal plane.

![[screenshot-2026-10-07_12-05-19_58934.png]]
#### Governing Equations & Quantitative Resolution

- **Initial Vector Resolution:**
  $$u_x = u \cos(\theta)$$
  $$u_y = u \sin(\theta)$$
- **Horizontal Dynamics ($a_x = 0$):**
  $$v_x = u \cos(\theta)$$
  $$x = (u \cos(\theta))t$$
- **Vertical Dynamics ($a_y = -9.81 \text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$):**
  $$v_y = u \sin(\theta) - gt$$
  $$y = (u \sin(\theta))t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$
  $$v_y^2 = (u \sin(\theta))^2 - 2gy$$


#### Protocol to Find Core Flight Values

1. **Time to Peak Apex ($t_{\text{peak}}$):** Set the instantaneous vertical velocity to zero ($v_y = 0$):
   $$0 = u \sin(\theta) - gt_{\text{peak}} \implies t_{\text{peak}} = \frac{u \sin(\theta)}{g}$$

2. **Maximum Vertical Height Attained ($y_{\text{max}}$):** Calculate using the vertical components equation:
   $$y_{\text{max}} = \frac{(u \sin(\theta))^2}{2g}$$

3. **Total Time of Flight ($t_{\text{total}}$):** For a launch and landing on flat ground, find the time taken for total vertical displacement to return to zero ($y = 0$):
   $$0 = (u \sin(\theta))t_{\text{total}} - \frac{1}{2}gt_{\text{total}}^2 \implies t_{\text{total}} = \frac{2u \sin(\theta)}{g}$$

4. **Total Horizontal Range ($x_{\text{max}}$):** Substitute the total flight time into the horizontal displacement equation:
   $$x_{\text{max}} = u \cos(\theta) \times t_{\text{total}} = u \cos(\theta) \times \frac{2u \sin(\theta)}{g} = \frac{u^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$$

5. **Instantaneous Velocity Vector at Time $t$:** Compute the independent component values at that specific timestamp ($v_x = u \cos(\theta)$ and $v_y = u \sin(\theta) - gt$). Recombine them to find the true velocity magnitude:
   $$v_{\text{instantaneous}} = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$$

## 4. Dynamics: Forces and Newton's Laws

Dynamics establishes the causal links between forces acting upon a physical system and the resulting structural state of motion.

### Newton's First Law of Motion
An object will maintain its current state of rest or continue moving at a constant uniform velocity unless acted upon by a non-zero net external resultant force.

### Newton's Second Law of Motion
The net external resultant force acting on a body is directly proportional to the rate of change of its linear momentum. For a system containing a constant, non-changing mass $m$:
$$\Sigma F = ma$$

### Newton's Third Law of Motion
When body A exerts a force on body B, body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force of identical type on body A. These forces must act on two different bodies.

### System Analysis and Free-Body Diagrams
A free-body diagram isolates a single object and displays every individual external force vector acting directly upon it. Common force vectors include:
* **Weight ($W = mg$):** Acts vertically downward through the center of gravity.
* **Normal Contact Force ($R$):** Acts perpendicular to the boundary surface.
* **Friction ($f$) or Drag ($D$):** Opposes the relative direction of motion.
* **Tension ($T$):** Pulls along the linear axis of a string or cable.

### Motion on an Inclined Plane
For an object resting or sliding along a plane inclined at an angle $\theta$ relative to the horizontal:
* Resolve weight parallel to the incline: 
  $$W_{\parallel} = mg \sin(	heta)$$
* Resolve weight perpendicular to the incline: 
  $$W_{\perp} = mg \cos(	heta)$$
* If the object is stationary or moving at a constant speed without accelerating, the forces are balanced:
  $$R = mg \cos(	heta)$$

## 5. Work, Energy, and Power

### Work Done
Work done measures the energy transferred by a force acting over a displacement. When the applied force acts at an angle $	heta$ to the displacement vector $\Delta s$:
$$W = F \Delta s \cos(\theta)$$

### Kinetic Energy
Kinetic energy ($E_k$) is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion. Derived from work done ($W = Fs = mas$) combined with the kinematic equation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ (assuming $u=0$):
$$E_k =\frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

### Gravitational Potential Energy
Gravitational potential energy ($\Delta E_{	ext{grav}}$) is the energy stored by an object due to its position within a uniform gravitational field:
$$\Delta E_{\text{grav}} = mgh$$

### Conservation of Energy
In an isolated system, total energy remains constant. Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one structural classification to another. In a frictionless system falling from rest:
$$mgh = rac{1}{2}mv^2$$

### Power
Power ($P$) is defined as the rate at which work is performed or energy is converted over time:
$$P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{\Delta E}{t}$$
For a mechanical system maintaining a constant velocity $v$ against a constant resistive force $F$:
$$P = Fv$$

### Efficiency
Efficiency measures the ratio of useful energy output to total energy input within a system:
$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{	\text{Useful Energy Output}}{\text{Total Energy Input}} = \frac{\text{Useful Power Output}}{\text{Total Power Input}}$$
