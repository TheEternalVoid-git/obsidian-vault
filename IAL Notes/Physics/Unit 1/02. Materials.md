### Density
Density ($\rho$) is defined as the mass per unit volume of a substance:
$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

### Upthrust and Archimedes' Principle
Upthrust is an upward force exerted by a fluid on any immersed or floating object. It arises due to the hydro-static pressure difference between the top and bottom faces of the object.

Archimedes' principle states that the upthrust force acting on an object immersed in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by that object:
$$U = m_{\text{fluid}}g = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{submerged}}g$$

For an object floating in static equilibrium:
$$\text{Weight of Object} = \text{Upthrust Force}$$
$$m_{\text{object}}g = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{submerged}}g$$

## 2. Fluid Flow and Viscosity

### Laminar vs. Turbulent Flow
* **Laminar Flow:** Adjacent layers of fluid slide smoothly past one another with minimal mixing. The velocity vector at any fixed spatial coordinate remains constant over time. Streamlines never cross.
* **Turbulent Flow:** Fluid layers mix abruptly, forming chaotic eddies and vortices. The velocity at a given point fluctuates continuously. This type of flow increases kinetic energy dissipation and drag.

### Viscosity
Viscosity ($\eta$) quantifies a fluid's internal resistance to flow or shearing forces. Highly viscous fluids flow slowly and exert significant drag forces on moving objects. Viscosity is highly temperature-dependent:
* **Liquids:** Viscosity decreases as temperature rises due to the reduction of intermolecular cohesive forces.
* **Gases:** Viscosity increases as temperature rises due to a higher frequency of molecular collisions.

### Stokes' Law
Stokes' law calculates the viscous drag force $F_d$ acting on a perfectly spherical object moving through a fluid under conditions of laminar flow:
$$F_d = 6\pi \eta r v$$
Where:
* $\eta$ is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid.
* $r$ is the radius of the sphere.
* $v$ is the instantaneous velocity of the sphere relative to the fluid.

### Terminal Velocity Derivation in a Viscous Fluid
Consider a solid sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho_s$ falling vertically through a fluid of density $\rho_f$ and viscosity $\eta$. As velocity increases, the viscous drag force increases until the forces acting on the sphere reach dynamic equilibrium.

#### 1. Set Up the Force Balance Equation
When the sphere reaches its constant terminal velocity $v_t$, the net acceleration drops to zero ($a = 0$):
$$\Sigma F = 0$$
$$\text{Weight} = \text{Upthrust} + \text{Viscous Drag}$$
$$W = U + F_d$$

#### 2. Substitute the Physical Variables
Substitute the geometric volume of a sphere ($V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$) and Stokes' law into the force balance equation:
$$\rho_s \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right)g = \rho_f \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right)g + 6\pi \eta r v_t$$

#### 3. Isolate the Drag Expression
$$\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right)g(\rho_s - \rho_f) = 6\pi \eta r v_t$$

#### 4. Solve for Terminal Velocity ($v_t$)
$$v_t = \frac{\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 g (\rho_s - \rho_f)}{6\pi \eta r}$$
$$v_t = \frac{2r^2 g(\rho_s - \rho_f)}{9\eta}$$

## 3. Solid Materials and Deformation

Solid mechanics investigates how solid structures deform when subjected to external tensile or compressive forces.

### Hooke's Law
Hooke's law states that the extension $\Delta x$ of a material is directly proportional to the applied tensile force $F$, provided the material does not exceed its limit of proportionality:
$$F = k\Delta x$$
Where $k$ is the stiffness constant of the sample.

### Stress, Strain, and the Young Modulus
Stiffness $k$ depends on the specific dimensions of a sample. To evaluate the fundamental properties of a material independent of its geometry, engineers use stress and strain.

#### Tensile Stress ($\sigma$)
Stress is the force applied per unit cross-sectional area:
$$\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$$
The SI unit for stress is the Pascal ($\text{Pa}$), where $1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ Nm}^{-2}$.

#### Tensile Strain ($\epsilon$)
Strain is the fractional extension of the material:
$$\epsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{x_0}$$
Where $x_0$ is the original unextended length. Strain is a dimensionless ratio.

#### The Young Modulus ($E$)
The Young modulus measures the stiffness of a material. It is defined as the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain within the linear section of a stress-strain graph:
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon} = \frac{\frac{F}{A}}{\frac{\Delta x}{x_0}} = \frac{Fx_0}{A\Delta x}$$

### Key Graph Transitions Explained

* **Limit of Proportionality (P):** Up to this coordinate, stress is directly proportional to strain. Hooke's law is obeyed precisely, meaning the gradient remains perfectly linear. The straight-line gradient of this specific region equals the Young Modulus ($E$) of the material.
* **Elastic Limit (E):** The maximal boundary threshold a material can support without sustaining permanent structural alteration. If the applied mechanical load is dropped before reaching this marker, the specimen returns to its pristine, original dimensions. Past this limit, plastic deformation initiates.
* **Yield Point (Y):** The explicit coordinate where the solid crystal lattice planes permanently slip past one another. The material flows or elongates rapidly along the horizontal strain axis with minimal or zero corresponding increase in mechanical tensile stress.
* **Ultimate Tensile Strength (U):** The absolute peak magnitude of stress the material can structurally bear. Beyond this threshold point, local cross-sectional narrowing—known as **necking**—takes hold, weakening the material's geometry.
* **Fracture Point (F):** The final operational failure point where internal atomic separations break completely, causing physical fracture.

### Material Classifications
* **Brittle Materials:** Exhibit high stiffness and strict linear elastic behavior directly up to their fracture point with little or no plastic deformation (e.g., glass, high-carbon steel).
* **Ductile Materials:** Exhibit a well-defined linear elastic region followed by extensive plastic deformation before fracturing, allowing them to be easily drawn into thin wire profiles (e.g., copper, mild steel).
* **Polymeric Materials:** Composed of long, entangled molecular cross-chains. They experience immense structural extensions, returning to original parameters through uncoiling mechanisms but displaying non-linear elastic paths without obeying Hooke's law (e.g., rubber).

## 5. Elastic Strain Energy

When a material is deformed, work is performed to change its intermolecular spacing. This work is stored within the material as elastic strain energy ($E_{\text{str}}$).

### Energy Calculation from Force-Extension Profiles
The total elastic strain energy stored corresponds to the area enclosed underneath the force-extension curve:
$$E_{\text{str}} = \int F \, d(\Delta x)$$

Within the linear elastic limit where Hooke's law ($F = k\Delta x$) holds true:
$$E_{\text{str}} = \frac{1}{2}F\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$$

### Energy Stored per Unit Volume
For a stress-strain curve, the area under the linear portion represents the elastic strain energy stored per unit volume ($V = A x_0$):
$$\text{Energy Density} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \epsilon$$
