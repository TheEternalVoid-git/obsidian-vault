# 02.  Electricity

## 1. Electric Current, Charge, and Drift Velocity

### Electric Current and Charge
Electric current $I$ is defined as the rate of flow of net electric charge $Q$ across a cross-sectional boundary:
$$I = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}$$
Charge is quantized; the charge carried by any system must be an integer multiple of the elementary charge $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$:
$$Q = ne$$

### Microscopic Model of Current: Drift Velocity
Inside a conductive material sample of cross-sectional area $A$ containing a charge carrier number density $n$ (carriers per cubic meter), the net current is linked to the average carrier drift velocity $v$ by:
$$I = nAve$$

## 2. Potential Difference, Resistance, and Resistivity

### Potential Difference and Electromotive Force (EMF)
* **Potential Difference ($V$):** The electrical energy converted into non-electrical forms (such as heat or light) per unit charge passing between two coordinates:
  $$V = \frac{W}{Q}$$
* **Electromotive Force ($\varepsilon$):** The non-electrical energy converted into electrical potential energy per unit charge supplied across the internal bounds of a power source.

### Ohm's Law and Resistance
The electrical resistance $R$ of a circuit component is defined by the ratio of potential difference to current:
$$R = \frac{V}{I}$$
Ohm's law states that for a metallic conductor maintained at a constant temperature, the current passing through it is directly proportional to the potential difference across its terminals ($V \propto I$), meaning $R$ stays fixed.

### Resistivity
Resistance depends on a component's specific dimensions. To isolate the foundational properties of a material independent of its geometry, engineers utilize resistivity ($\rho$):
$$R = \frac{
\rho l}{A}$$
Where $l$ is the length of the conductor and $A$ is its cross-sectional area. Resistivity is highly temperature-dependent:
* **Metallic Conductors:** Resistivity increases with temperature. Thermal vibrations of the lattice ions increase, causing a higher frequency of collisions with moving conduction electrons, which impedes flow.
* **Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) Thermistors:** Resistivity decreases rapidly as temperature rises. The thermal energy breaks covalent bonds within the semiconductor, liberating a massive number of additional charge carriers and driving up the carrier density $n$.

## 3. Circuit Analysis Techniques

### Kirchhoff's First Law
Based on the fundamental law of **conservation of charge**, the total electric charge entering any circuit junction must exactly equal the total charge exiting that junction:
$$\Sigma I_{\text{in}} = \Sigma I_{\text{out}}$$

### Kirchhoff's Second Law
Based on the fundamental law of **conservation of energy**, the algebraic sum of the electromotive forces ($\varepsilon$) around any closed circuit loop must exactly equal the algebraic sum of the potential drops ($IR$) around that same loop:
$$\Sigma \varepsilon = \Sigma IR$$

### Series and Parallel Networks

| Operational Metric          | Series Circuit Layout                | Parallel Circuit Layout                                                     |
| :-------------------------- | :----------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Current Vector Profile**  | $I_{\text{total}} = I_1 = I_2 = I_3$ | $I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$                                        |
| **Voltage Drop Allocation** | $V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$ | $V_{\text{total}} = V_1 = V_2 = V_3$                                        |
| **Equivalent Resistance**   | $R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3$ | $\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} =\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}$ |

## 4. Internal Resistance

Real chemical cells possess an inherent internal resistance ($r$) due to the material constraints and chemical interfaces within the cell. 

### The EMF Loop Equation
The total electromotive force generated by a cell is split between driving current through the external circuit load ($R$) and overcoming its internal resistance ($r$):
$$\varepsilon = I(R + r) = IR + Ir$$
Since the terminal potential difference $V$ across the external load equals $IR$, the equation scales to:
$$V = \varepsilon - Ir$$
Where $Ir$ represents the internal **lost volts** dropped across the cell.

## 5. Potential Dividers and Electrical Power

### Potential Divider Circuits
Potential dividers distribute voltage across series components in direct proportion to their resistance values. For two resistors $R_1$ and $R_2$ arranged in series across an input voltage $V_{\text{in}}$, the output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ tapped across $R_2$ is governed by:
$$V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} 
\right)$$
Replacing $R_2$ with a sensor such as an LDR (Light Dependent Resistor) or an NTC thermistor creates an automated control circuit that modulates $V_{\text{out}}$ in response to ambient environmental intensity shifts.

### Electrical Power Expressions
Power $P$ measures the rate of electrical energy transfer within a circuit component. Combining the core definition $P = IV$ with Ohm's law ($V = IR$) yields three equivalent expressions:
$$P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$
