# 01. Waves

## 1. Wave Classification and Behavior

Waves transfer energy through a medium or vacuum without transferring physical matter.

### Mechanical vs. Electromagnetic Waves
* **Mechanical Waves:** Require a physical material medium to propagate (e.g., sound waves, water waves, seismic waves).
* **Electromagnetic Waves:** Oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through a vacuum at a constant speed:
  $$c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ ms}^{-1}$$

### Longitudinal vs. Transverse Waves
* **Longitudinal Waves:** Particle vibrations oscillate parallel to the direction of wave energy transfer. They consist of compressions (high-pressure regions) and rarefactions (low-pressure regions). Sound waves are strictly longitudinal.
* **Transverse Waves:** Particle vibrations oscillate perpendicular to the direction of wave energy transfer. Electromagnetic waves and surface water waves are transverse.

### The Wave Equation
For all continuous periodic waves, the velocity $v$ is the product of frequency $f$ and wavelength $\lambda$:
$$v = f\lambda$$

## 2. Polarization and Wave Properties

### Polarization
Polarization is a phenomenon unique to transverse waves. It restricts the oscillations of the wave to a single plane perpendicular to the direction of energy propagation. 

If unpolarized light is passed through a vertical polarizing filter, the output intensity is halved. Rotating a secondary filter (analyzer) by an angle $\theta$ relative to the first varies the transmitted intensity according to Malus's law:
$$I = I_0 \cos^2(\theta)$$
When $\theta = 90^\circ$ (crossed polarizers), total intensity output drops to zero. Longitudinal waves cannot be polarized because their oscillations are already confined to a single dimension parallel to the propagation path.

### Pulse-Echo Techniques
Pulse-echo systems determine distances by transmitting brief wave pulses and tracking the elapsed reflection interval $\Delta t$:
$$\text{Distance to Target} = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{2}$$
The duration of the individual pulse sets the minimum resolvable feature boundary, while the time between pulses sets the maximum operating distance without echo overlap.

## 3. Superposition and Interference

### The Principle of Superposition
When two or more co-propagating waves meet at a spatial coordinate, the resultant instantaneous vector displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual wave displacements:
$$y_{\text{total}} = y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$$
### Interference
Interference is the structural consequence of superposition between coherent wave sources. Coherence requires a constant phase relationship and identical operating frequencies.

* **Constructive Interference:** Waves arrive completely in-phase. Path difference is an integer multiple of wavelengths:
  $$\text{Path Difference} = n\lambda \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$
* **Destructive Interference:** Waves arrive completely out-of-phase ($180^\circ$ or $\pi$ radians). Path difference is a half-integer multiple of wavelengths:
  $$\text{Path Difference} = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$

### Young's Double-Slit Equation
When coherent light passes through two slits separated by a distance $d$, it diffracts and forms an interference pattern of bright and dark fringes on a parallel screen a distance $D$ away. The fringe separation $x$ is given by:
$$x = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$

### Diffraction Gratings
A diffraction grating splits incoming light into independent wavefront paths via thousands of closely spaced parallel lines per millimeter. The structural condition for bright, constructive interference maxima is governed by:
$$d \sin(\theta) = n\lambda$$
Where $d$ is the slit spacing separation distance ($d = \frac{1}{\text{lines per metre}}$), $\theta$ is the angular deviation from the central zeroth-order maximum, and $n$ is the numeric order of the fringe ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$).

## 4. Stationary (Standing) Waves

Stationary waves are formed when two progressive waves of identical frequency, wavelength, and amplitude travel in opposite directions through the same spatial region and superimpose. Unlike progressive waves, stationary waves store vibrational energy within local pockets rather than propagating it through space.

### Key Boundary Coordinates
* **Nodes:** Points of zero oscillation amplitude. Formed via continuous destructive interference.
* **Anti-nodes:** Points of maximum oscillation amplitude. Formed via continuous constructive interference.

### Resonant Modes on a Tensioned String (Length L)
On a string fixed firmly at both boundaries, nodes are locked at the ends. This establishes distinct geometric harmonic frequencies:

![[screenshot-2026-10-07_12-11-24_61564.png]]

The underlying velocity $v$ of a transverse wave travelling on a tensioned string depends on tension $T$ and mass per unit length $\mu$:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$
Combining this with fundamental boundary configurations ($\lambda_1 = 2L$) defines the fundamental operational frequency:
$$f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$

## 5. Refraction and Quantum Nature of Light

### Refractive Index and Snell's Law
Refraction is the change in direction of a wave due to a shift in its propagation velocity when transitioning across a boundary between media of differing optical densities. The absolute refractive index $n$ is defined by:
$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$
Snell's law governs the angular translation tracking at an interface:
$$n_1 \sin(\theta_1) = n_2 \sin(\theta_2)$$

### Total Internal Reflection (TIR)
When a wave travels from a high refractive index medium into a lower refractive index medium ($n_1 > n_2$), the light refracts away from the normal line. The **critical angle** $\theta_c$ is the specific angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is exactly $90^\circ$:
$$\sin(\theta_c) = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
If the angle of incidence exceeds $\theta_c$, refraction ceases completely, and the entire wave energy undergoes total internal reflection back inside the primary medium.

### The Photoelectric Effect and Photon Energy
The photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation. Light is composed of discrete quanta of energy called photons. The energy $E$ of a single photon is directly proportional to its frequency $f$:
$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$
Where $h = 6.63 	\times 10^{-34} \text{ Js}$ is Planck's constant.

According to Einstein's photoelectric equation, an incoming photon transfers its energy entirely to a single surface electron. Energy must overcome a minimum boundary threshold called the **work function** ($\Phi$) to liberate that electron:
$$hf = \Phi + E_{k(\text{max})}$$
Where $E_{k(\text{max})}$ is the maximum kinetic energy of the escaping photoelectron. If $f$ drops below the threshold frequency $f_0 = \frac{\Phi}{h}$, zero emission occurs, regardless of the incoming light's wave intensity.
